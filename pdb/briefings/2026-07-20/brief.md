Of that seaborne flow, around one barrel in four — 17 to 20 million barrels a day, about a fifth of all the oil humanity uses — passes through the Strait of Hormuz, the waterway between Iran and Oman that narrows to about 21 nautical miles, with inbound and outbound shipping lanes just two miles wide apiece. History says attacks on Gulf shipping usually fail to stop it — which is precisely why this episode stands out. During the 1984–88 "Tanker War" phase of the Iran-Iraq war, the two sides attacked roughly 450 merchant ships over four years, yet traffic never stopped: insurance premiums rose, crews drew danger pay, and oil prices actually fell across the period because the world was oversupplied. The better calibrator for price is Iraq's August 1990 invasion of Kuwait, which roughly doubled oil prices in two months on fear of lost supply, then gave the entire move back within months when the feared shortage never materialized. The current crisis has already traced both halves of that pattern once: Brent spot ran from the low $70s before the war to $138 at the April 7 peak (US Energy Information Administration daily data), collapsed to $68.53 on July 2 during the June truce, and has retraced to roughly $88–90 in the nine trading days since President Trump declared the truce over on July 7. A genuinely sustained closure of a fifth of world oil supply would put prices well above April's peak; conversely, a new truce or a recovery to more than 50 transits a day (checkable daily via ship-tracking data) would very likely (80–95%) send Brent back toward the $70s, exactly as it did in June.

### 3. US tariff regime hits a legal cliff this week: Section 122 blanket 10% expires July 24, Section 301 replacement not yet finalized

- **Status:** confirmed · **Confidence:** 0.85 (verifier-revised from 0.80) · **Beat:** supply-trade
- **What happened:** The blanket 10% tariff imposed February 24 under Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974 — a balance-of-payments authority statutorily capped at 150 days — sunsets this Friday, July 24, and cannot be extended without Congress. The intended replacement, built on Section 301 of the same act (an unfair-trade-practices authority), would set duties of 12.5% on 46 economies including China, India, Japan, South Korea and Vietnam, and 10% on 14 others, based on forced-labor findings announced June 2; the Federal Register confirms no final determination has been published as of July 20. One correction from verification: the widely reported "July 20 completion deadline" for that determination could not be confirmed in any primary source, so the accurate framing is that the administrative record closed July 16 and the only binding date is Friday's statutory sunset — and the "record June port volumes" front-loading claim was cut as unsourced. A separate 25% Section 301 duty on Brazil (with substantial carve-outs for beef, orange juice, civil aircraft and energy) takes effect Wednesday, July 22. If the broad action is not in place by Friday, importers revert temporarily to the old default rates — roughly 2% on average — plus surviving steel/aluminum and China-specific duties. European Union goods are insulated, having moved to a 15% all-inclusive ceiling on July 1 under the US-EU deal.
- **Why it matters:** This is the single largest scheduled change in the applied United States tariff schedule this year, covering most of roughly $3 trillion in annual goods imports. A gap between Friday's expiry and the replacement would create a short low-tariff window that every importer with cargo on the water can see. And unlike Section 122, Section 301 has no rate cap and no time limit — the 12.5% proposal is a floor that can be ratcheted. Judgment: it is likely (55–80%) the administration finalizes the Section 301 action within days of the deadline rather than allowing a prolonged gap; resolution is observable in the Federal Register by July 24.

**The mechanics.** The United States buys about $3.3 trillion of goods from abroad each year — roughly $9 billion crossing the border every day, making it the world's largest importer, taking in around one-eighth of everything traded globally. Tariffs are collected at the moment goods clear customs, so the rate in force on any given day is worth real money: at the current blanket 10%, each day represents roughly $900 million in duty. For decades that hardly mattered — the average United States tariff had drifted down to about 2%, and monthly customs collections ran near $7.5 billion as recently as early 2025. The 2025–26 tariff regime quadrupled that, peaking at $33 billion in October 2025. But blanket tariffs rest on borrowed legal authority: the Constitution gives tariff power to Congress, and presidents act through delegated statutes, each with built-in tripwires. The statute carrying the current 10% (Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974, a balance-of-payments tool) is fast to invoke but self-destructs after 150 days — a clock that runs out this Friday, July 24, and only Congress can extend. Its intended replacement (Section 301, an unfair-trade-practices tool proposing 12.5% on 46 economies and 10% on 14 others) is slow — investigation, comments, hearings — but has no rate cap and no expiration. The government is mid-handoff between the two: the administrative record closed July 16, yet no final decision has been published. If the second leg is not down by Friday, most imports revert for however long the gap lasts to the old roughly-2% default rates — an arbitrage visible to every importer with cargo on the water.

Two episodes calibrate expectations. The only prior blanket import surcharge in modern American history was Richard Nixon's, imposed August 15, 1971 at the same 10%: it lasted barely four months, lifted that December after a currency deal, and an alarmed Congress then wrote the very 150-day fuse now burning into the 1974 Trade Act. More recently and more tellingly, when the Supreme Court struck down the previous emergency-powers tariffs in early 2026, the administration re-imposed the blanket 10% under Section 122 within days — evidence that these handoffs get executed fast when the White House wants continuity. The residue of that episode sits in the Treasury's own books: net customs receipts in June 2026 were minus $25.6 billion, as refunds of the struck-down tariffs flowed out faster than new duties came in — a reminder that tariffs imposed on shaky authority can be unwound, expensively. The 2018–19 China tariff rounds taught importers the other half of the game: front-run every effective date, so any gap after Friday should produce a visible surge of customs entries. It is likely (55–80%) the replacement is finalized within days of the deadline; the question resolves in the Federal Register by July 24.

### 4. IEA: full Chinese rare-earth export curbs would put $6.5 trillion of annual downstream production at risk, as Beijing signals active enforcement

- **Status:** confirmed (that the estimates were published and the enforcement actions occurred; the $6.5T itself is a model scenario) · **Confidence:** 0.85 (verifier-revised from 0.75, status upgraded from reported) · **Beat:** supply-trade
- **What happened:** The International Energy Agency's critical-minerals assessment, published July 16, estimates that full implementation of China's rare-earth export restrictions would jeopardize about $6.5 trillion per year of downstream production outside China — automotive, defense, high-tech and energy hardest hit, with the United States and Europe accounting for nearly half the exposure to the seven heavy rare earths controlled since April 2025. Battery-grade graphite curbs alone would put over $300 billion at risk annually. China's expanded controls — announced October 2025 and extended extraterritorially to foreign-made products containing Chinese rare earths — are suspended only until November 10, 2026. In parallel, Chinese enforcement is visibly tightening: the Ministry of Commerce formalized a public whistleblower mechanism for strategic-mineral export violations (Announcement No. 26 of 2026, effective July 1), two Japanese nationals were detained in Dalian over alleged rare-earth smuggling (in May, the verifier notes, not a fresh July action), and in June Beijing added 10 United States entities — including rare-earth miners MP Materials and USA Rare Earth — to its own export-control list.
- **Why it matters:** These are agency estimates, not realized losses — the $6.5 trillion is a claim about a scenario, from an agency with a diversification agenda, and the controls' worst provisions remain suspended. But the structure is the point: China processes up to 90% of the world's rare earths (it mines about 69%) and effectively all heavy rare earths, a single-supplier concentration with few historical parallels — the 2010 China-Japan embargo, at far smaller scale, sent prices up severalfold within months. Base rate: China has so far used these controls as negotiating leverage, suspending or licensing around them rather than imposing maximal cutoffs, so full implementation in November is unlikely (20–45%) — but the enforcement drumbeat raises the credibility of the threat, which is itself the leverage. The proposed multilateral stockpiling effort the IEA costs at roughly $900 million for 11 high-risk materials (a proposal, not an existing program) would cover a trivial fraction of the exposure.

**The mechanics.** Rare earths are seventeen metallic elements that are not actually rare in the ground — they are rare in usable form, because separating them from ore and from each other is a filthy, capital-hungry chemical process that most countries spent thirty years happily outsourcing to China. The result is one of the most lopsided leverage ratios in the world economy: global rare-earth mining is a market measured in the low tens of billions of dollars a year, yet the International Energy Agency now estimates that a full Chinese export cutoff would put roughly $6.5 trillion of annual downstream production at risk — car plants, defense lines, wind turbines, robotics — because a few kilograms of rare-earth magnet sit inside every electric-vehicle motor and roughly 400 kilograms of rare-earth material go into a single F-35 fighter jet, with no drop-in substitute. China mines about 69% of the world's supply but processes up to 90% of it, and for the "heavy" rare earths that make magnets survive high temperatures (dysprosium, terbium), its separation share is effectively 100%. That is a chokepoint of the same order as the Strait of Hormuz for oil — except one government owns the strait, and in October 2025 it wrote itself rules claiming authority over foreign-made products anywhere on Earth that contain Chinese rare earths. Those rules are suspended, under a trade truce, only until November 10, 2026.

Two episodes calibrate what happens when Beijing squeezes. In September 2010, after Japan arrested a Chinese trawler captain near disputed islands, China quietly halted rare-earth shipments to Japan for about two months: prices of some elements rose as much as tenfold into 2011, Japan bankrolled an Australian producer and redesigned products to use less, and by 2015 prices had crashed and China's mining share had slid from about 97% toward today's 69% — but the processing monopoly never broke. The second episode just happened: on April 4, 2025 China imposed licensing on seven heavy rare earths and the magnets made from them, and within two months its worldwide magnet exports collapsed from 6,357 tonnes in January to 1,238 tonnes in May, a five-year low; Ford halted Explorer production in Chicago and European suppliers warned of shutdowns before a June truce restored license flows and exports rebounded above pre-control levels by August. The lesson cuts both ways: the weapon works within weeks, but Beijing has so far used it as a dial — throttle, extract concessions, re-license — not a switch, because every month of cutoff accelerates the $65 billion in public money now funding non-Chinese alternatives. The July 16 report is the first official attempt to price the switch, and the detentions, whistleblower hotline, and entity listings of mid-2026 are Beijing making sure everyone believes it still has its hand on the dial as the November deadline approaches.
